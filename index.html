Judaism
Parashat Ha'azinu — Deuteronomy 32:1–52
Read

This week's Torah portion is Moses's final address to the Israelites, delivered not as law but as a long poem recited just before his death. It traces a recurring arc: a people cared for and provisioned grows secure, drifts from the commitments that sustained it, and meets the natural consequences of that drift — before a path back to repair opens. The portion closes with Moses ordered up Mount Nebo, to view the land he will not enter.

Core principle

Comfort and security tend to erode the commitments that produced them. Sustaining a community's values takes active retelling and remembering — it doesn't happen by default once things are stable.

What commitment in your own life do you keep mostly out of habit rather than active conviction — and would it hold up under real strain?

09.15
Catholicism
25th Sunday, Ordinary Time (Year A) — Matthew 20:1–16a
Today

This Sunday's Gospel is the parable of the workers in the vineyard: a landowner hires laborers at dawn, then again at nine, noon, three, and five o'clock, promising each "what is just." At day's end he pays everyone the same full wage, starting with the last hired. Those who worked the whole day grumble at the equal pay; the owner replies that he has wronged no one — he paid what was agreed — and asks whether they resent his generosity. The first reading (Isaiah 55:6–9) frames the same idea from the other side, describing a God whose ways and thoughts run higher than human ones, especially in extending mercy.

Core principle

Fairness measured by generosity toward need doesn't subtract from fairness measured by effort — but it can still feel like a loss, because human accounting instinctively compares itself to others rather than checking only what it was promised.

Where have you resented someone else's good fortune not because you came up short, but because it exceeded what their effort seemed to warrant?

How to read this page. Each entry names its source the way that tradition cites itself — chapter and verse, a portion's name, a lectionary date — so the full text is easy to find elsewhere. The summary and principle are written in plain, non-devotional language on purpose: not a claim about what's true, just what the passage says and what it's asking of a reader.
