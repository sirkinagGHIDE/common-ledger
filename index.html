09.21
Judaism
Shabbat Chol HaMoed Sukkot — Exodus 33:12–34:26; Haftarah: Ezekiel 38:18–39:16
Read

The Torah reading for the Shabbat that falls during the intermediate days (Chol HaMoed) of Sukkot returns to Exodus, just after the incident of the golden calf. Moses asks to see God's glory directly; he is told no one can see God's face and live, and is instead placed in the cleft of a rock so he can see only God's back as God passes by, proclaiming a formula of attributes — compassionate, gracious, slow to anger, abundant in kindness and faithfulness. The covenant is renewed and a new set of tablets is cut to replace the ones Moses broke. On this same Shabbat, congregations also read the book of Ecclesiastes (Kohelet) aloud, whose refrain that everything is fleeting is paired deliberately with a festival built around dwelling in a temporary hut; the haftarah, from Ezekiel, describes a future invasion repelled by forces beyond human control.

Core principle

A request for total, direct access to someone or something beyond you is often answered instead with a partial view and a description of character, offered over time — and that partial view, not the full access originally asked for, is what actually has to be trusted.

Where have you had to build trust in someone or something from partial glimpses of their character, rather than the full and direct view you originally wanted?

TUE
09.22
Catholicism
Roman Rite Sunday lectionary, Sep 27 — Amos 6:1a,4–7; Psalm 146; 1 Timothy 6:11–16; Luke 16:19–31
Read

The readings for the Sunday closest to this week pair a warning from the prophet Amos against those who lounge in comfort while the surrounding society comes apart with the Gospel's parable of a rich man and the beggar Lazarus. In the parable, a wealthy man feasts lavishly every day while Lazarus lies starving and sore-covered at his gate, unnoticed; after both die, their situations reverse completely, and when the rich man asks that someone be sent to warn his still-living brothers, he is told no — if they won't listen to the moral instruction they already have, a dead man returning to speak to them wouldn't persuade them either. Read alongside this, Paul's letter to Timothy urges him to keep pursuing righteousness and steadiness rather than the kind of wealth the other two readings hold up for criticism.

Core principle

Proximity to someone else's suffering carries a claim on you; choosing not to see what's directly in front of you doesn't make you innocent of it.

What suffering is close enough for you to see clearly, that familiarity has made easy to stop noticing?

WED
09.23
Islam
Sahih al-Bukhari 1 (also Hadith 1 of 40 Hadith Nawawi) — narrated by 'Umar ibn al-Khattab
Read

This hadith opens Sahih al-Bukhari and is traditionally placed first in collections of foundational hadith. 'Umar ibn al-Khattab reports the Prophet saying that actions are judged by the intentions behind them, and each person will be credited only for what they intended. The hadith then gives a worked example: someone who migrates (makes hijrah) for the sake of God and His messenger has a migration that counts as such, while someone who undertakes the same outward journey for worldly gain, or to marry a particular woman, has a migration that counts only as reaching that worldly thing. The two migrations look identical from the outside; the hadith locates their entire value in the motive that isn't visible to an observer.

Core principle

Two people can perform the identical outward act, and it is not the same act — what it actually amounts to is set by the private motive underneath it, not by what a witness can see.

Think of something you did recently that looked, from the outside, like a single clear act — what mix of motives was actually underneath it?

THU
09.24
Buddhism
Dhammapada, Chapter 2: Appamādavagga (Heedfulness), verses 21–32
Today

The second chapter of the Dhammapada opens with a stark equation: heedfulness (appamāda — sustained, careful attention) is the path to the deathless, while heedlessness is the path to death; the heedful, it says, do not die, while the heedless are already as good as dead. The verses that follow build the case through comparison rather than argument — the heedful person is described as energetic, mindful, and self-controlled, with a glory that steadily increases; as wide awake among the sleeping, advancing like a swift horse leaving a weak, spent one behind; as a mountaineer looking down from a summit at people still struggling on the sorrowful ground below. The chapter closes with the heedful practitioner pictured advancing like a fire, burning away every fetter, large and small.

Core principle

Sustained, careful attention to what you're actually doing isn't a minor virtue alongside others — it's the difference that decides whether everything else you value is gained or lost.

Where in your life are you coasting on a habit or intention formed once, rather than staying genuinely attentive to it now?
