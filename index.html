WEEK OF OCT 5–11, 2026·4 of 7 entries delivered
MON
10.05
Judaism
Parashat Bereshit — Genesis 1:1–6:8; Haftarah (Shabbat Machar Chodesh): 1 Samuel 20:18–42
Read

Parashat Bereshit opens the Torah's annual reading cycle again right after the holiday season ends, covering Genesis 1:1–6:8: the six days of creation followed by a seventh of rest, the placement of Adam and Eve in Eden under a single prohibition they go on to break, and the birth of their sons Cain and Abel, whose jealousy over an accepted offering ends in the first murder. The portion closes by tracing the genealogy from Adam through ten generations to Noah, ending on the verse that humanity's wickedness had grown so great that God resolved to act. Because this Shabbat falls the day before Rosh Chodesh, the new-moon observance, the haftarah is the Machar Chodesh reading from 1 Samuel: David, suspecting Saul means to kill him, has his friend Jonathan test Saul's intentions by covering for his absence from the new-moon feast, with a prearranged signal of arrows to tell David whether it is safe to return or time to flee.

Core principle

A family bond strained by jealousy can go either way — left unexamined, as with Cain, it ends in violence against a brother; recognized and acted on, as with Jonathan, it means risking a father's own anger to protect someone chosen as a friend.

Where have you let jealousy or resentment toward someone close to you go unexamined, the way Cain's did, rather than catching it early and choosing loyalty instead, the way Jonathan did?

TUE
10.06
Catholicism
28th Sunday in Ordinary Time (Oct 11), Year C — Isaiah 25:6–10a; Psalm 23:1–6; 2 Timothy 2:8–13; Luke 17:11–19
Read

The first reading, from Isaiah, pictures God spreading a feast on a mountain for every nation, tearing away the veil that covers all peoples, and wiping away every face's tears as death itself is finally destroyed. The responsorial psalm answers with Psalm 23's shepherd, who leads through green pastures and dark valleys alike and sets a table even "in the sight of my foes." The second reading, from 2 Timothy, has Paul writing from prison that enduring hardship for the sake of others is bound up with eventually sharing in what Christ secured, and that even unfaithfulness on a person's part doesn't make the deal void from God's side. The Gospel tells of ten people with leprosy whom Jesus heals on the road; only one — a Samaritan, the outsider among them — turns back to give thanks, and Jesus asks aloud where the other nine went.

Core principle

Being helped and acknowledging it are two separate acts, and the second one isn't automatic — it tends to come, this story suggests, most readily from whoever has the least built-in claim to belonging.

Of the people who have helped you most, which ones did you actually go back and thank — and did it correlate with how close to you they were, or with something else?

WED
10.07
Islam
Hadith of Abu Hurayrah — Sahih al-Bukhari 6018, Sahih Muslim 47 (An-Nawawi's Forty Hadith, no. 15)
Read

Abu Hurayrah, the most prolific narrator in the hadith literature, reports that the Prophet Muhammad said: "Whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day should speak good or remain silent; whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day should be generous to his neighbor; and whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day should be generous to his guest." The hadith is recorded in both Bukhari and Muslim and ties three separate behaviors — guarding one's speech, treating a neighbor well, and welcoming a guest — to the same stated condition of belief, rather than listing them as unrelated good manners. Commentators read the repeated framing as a test anyone can apply to themselves: each clause names something easy to let slide in the moment, and ties letting it slide to a claim about character rather than circumstance.

Core principle

Three ordinary, low-stakes moments — what you say when you could stay quiet instead, how you treat the person next door, how you treat someone temporarily in your care — are proposed here as truer tests of character than any high-stakes moment, because nothing forces you to get them right.

Which of the three — your speech when silence was free, your patience with a neighbor, your generosity to a guest — would least survive an honest accounting this week?

THU
10.08
Buddhism
Dhammapada, Chapter 4 — Pupphavagga ("The Flowers"), verses 44–59
Today

The fourth chapter of the Dhammapada, a collection of verses attributed to the Buddha, takes its name from a run of similes built on flowers, bees, and garlands. It opens by comparing the trained disciple, who examines the teaching and the world with care, to a skilled florist choosing blossoms, and includes the chapter's best-known image: a bee that draws nectar from a flower without harming its color or scent, offered as a model for how a person should move through a village gathering only what they need. Several verses warn that fine words preached without matching conduct are like a beautiful flower that carries no scent. The chapter closes with its most quoted figure — a fragrant lotus blooming out of a rubbish heap discarded by the roadside — used to describe how clear understanding can still arise amid the ordinary confusion surrounding it.

Core principle

However unpromising or ordinary your surroundings, clarity and decent conduct can still take root in them — what matters is not the setting but whether your actions end up matching your words.

Where in your own ordinary routine — not some dramatic setting, just the roadside rubbish heap of daily life — could something as out of place and fragrant as that lotus still manage to grow this week?

How to read this page. Each entry names its source the way that tradition cites itself — chapter and verse, a portion's name, a lectionary date — so the full text is easy to find elsewhere.
